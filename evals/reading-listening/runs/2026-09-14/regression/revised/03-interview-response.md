Q1: What reading format do you prefer, and why?

Illustrative model answer:

I prefer printed books, especially when I’m reading a novel. A paper book makes it easier for me to settle into the story because I’m not tempted to check messages on the same device. For example, I can leave my phone in another room and read a chapter before bed without switching to something else. I also like being able to flip back a few pages when I forget a character’s name. Digital books are useful when I’m traveling, since I can carry several at once, but at home I’d still choose paper. It helps me give one book my full attention.

Q2: How have your reading habits changed?

Illustrative model answer:

I used to read mainly whatever my teachers assigned, but now I choose more books for myself. The biggest change is that I read out of curiosity instead of just preparing for an exam. For instance, after seeing an old railway station in my town, I might look for a book about how railways changed cities. That gives me a question I actually want answered, so I’m more likely to keep reading. I also read in shorter sessions than I did at school. Even twenty minutes on a busy evening can be satisfying when the topic is something I’ve chosen.

Q3: Should schools encourage students to read fiction?

Illustrative model answer:

Yes, I think schools should encourage fiction, although students should have some choice about what they read. A story gives them a reason to consider how another person understands the same situation. For example, a character who seems selfish at first might become more understandable once the reader learns what that person is afraid of. Discussing that change can teach students to support an interpretation with details instead of making a quick judgment. I wouldn’t make every class read only one kind of novel, though. Offering a few different books could make the discussion more engaging while keeping that focus on careful reading.

Q4: How might technology change reading in the future?

Illustrative model answer:

I think technology will make it easier to move between reading and listening. Someone might read a few pages at home and then continue with the audio version while walking to class. That could help people fit longer books into a busy day without starting over each time they change activities. I also expect more tools that explain an unfamiliar word in its context. The risk is that readers could rely on summaries so much that they skip the book’s actual argument or story. So I’d want technology to help people stay with a text, rather than constantly pull them away from it.
