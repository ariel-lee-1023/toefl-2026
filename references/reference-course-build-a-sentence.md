## Corrections that change coaching decisions

- **Question punctuation is not an instruction to invert every clause.** In the original example “Do you know where the shuttle stops?”, only the outer question uses `Do + you`. The embedded clause keeps `the shuttle + stops`. In “Who called?”, `Who` is the subject; no auxiliary is required for the neutral subject question. Emphatic questions can behave differently, so explain the actual construction.
- **Opposite prompt/response sentence types are a course heuristic.** The lesson's claim is not verified here as a universal ETS constraint. Use the supplied frame and punctuation; never reject an otherwise valid item solely because both turns are questions.
- **Unused pieces have no assumed grammatical category.** The lesson emphasizes wrong verb forms; the historical Guide synthesis includes negation and conjunction examples. Eliminate pieces through agreement, complementation, syntax, and meaning, without assigning either source a frequency claim.
- **Context is still relevant.** It may not determine the first tile, but a complete response must fit the exchange. Do not revise pronouns or insert words merely to make it sound more conversational.
- **SVO and overt subjects are useful starting patterns, not universal laws.** English includes subject questions, intransitive clauses, imperatives with understood subjects, and other structures. Do not create a universal English validator from this lesson. Agreement also does not mean all clauses must share a tense; reported clauses and conditionals need construction-specific analysis.
- **Classify `that` by syntax.** A relative clause modifies an antecedent and contains a role linked to it; a content clause supplies a proposition; demonstrative `that` points to a referent and can be a determiner or stand alone. Reporting-verb presence is a clue, not a necessary or sufficient test. Original contrasts: “The note that she copied vanished”; “The claim that she resigned surprised us”; “That note vanished.” The content clause in the middle example follows a noun, demonstrating why the reporting-verb shortcut fails.

Treat this as a context-supported reading correction, not permission to alter an actual supplied `no` tile.
