## Choose a reading mode

**Exam-focused:** skim for topic and paragraph roles; read the question to identify the requested relationship; scan for the relevant idea or paraphrase; then slow down over the evidence and its context. A question about a consequence may point to “enabled” rather than repeat the question's vocabulary. Avoid rereading everything for each detail, but expand the evidence window whenever a pronoun, condition, or contrast requires it. Full reading first and question-first scanning are both usable; choose based on performance and available time.

**Learning-focused:** reread a sentence or paragraph even if no past question tested it. Reconstruct its meaning, connect its references, explain its role, and compare close paraphrases. Familiar content is useful here; accurate recall of familiar answers is not a fresh proficiency measure. Course advice to skip untested difficult sentences belongs to exam pacing, not every study session.

## Complete the Words

Read the whole short text for topic and relationships. For each gap, combine the visible letters and exact missing-letter count with the grammatical role, agreement, tense, collocation, and wider context. Fill easy gaps to expose more context, then reread the completed text. A natural phrase can suggest a candidate; it does not override incompatible letters or syntax. Ask the learner which clue supports a completion rather than supplying the word immediately. In learning mode, acknowledge defensible alternatives and repair ambiguous prompts.

The course's grammar/collocation/context sequence is a flexible teaching method. Its claim that each module takes roughly nine minutes with fifteen questions is not the current section specification. Use current ETS mechanics and the live timer.

## Match meaning and structure

| Target | Useful action | What makes a tempting answer fail |
|---|---|---|
| Detail/evidence | Find the idea requested, not merely a shared noun; inspect the complete proposition | A true fact answers a different question or belongs to another event |
| Main idea | Compress the whole text's scope and what it says about that scope | A correct example covers only one paragraph; a broad claim adds material |
| Vocabulary | Substitute a simpler meaning and check the sentence's sense and grammar | A topic-related word, another dictionary sense, or a loose associate changes the meaning |
| Inference | State the premises and the smallest supported conclusion | An extra assumption, reversed cause, or unjustified certainty is needed |
| Purpose/function | Name what the detail does for the larger explanation | Retelling an example does not explain its function; a neutral description becomes an argument |
| Reference | Replace a pronoun or demonstrative with its candidate referent | The nearest noun matches number but not the intended meaning |
| EXCEPT | Check each choice against its own evidence; select the unsupported one | Recognition is mistaken for support or the question's negation is missed |
| Sentence insertion | Check backward reference and the connection to what follows | A locally plausible placement breaks the next sentence's logic |

Sentence insertion and some named question subtypes are course teaching coverage. Do not promise their frequency on current forms without an official specification or sample confirming it. The course's “rare,” “first question,” and timing estimates are observations/suggestions.

## Preserve the proposition

For a close paraphrase, compare agent, action, object, time, negation, causal direction, scope, degree of certainty, and conditions. Use the dimensions relevant to the item, not a nine-part questionnaire every turn. A change from “fees depend on weight” to “fees remain fixed” fails even though both mention fees. Strong wording is acceptable when the source supports it; do not train automatic rejection of “all” or automatic preference for neutral choices.

## Source-quality notes

The transcript occasionally renders EXCEPT as “accept,” routes as “roots,” and contains broken repetitions and sentences. Treat these as transcription defects, not terminology. Colour references such as “purple words” depend on missing slides. Claims that there is no slang, certain subjects never appear, or inference needs “no logic” are teaching simplifications. Teach supported reasoning and current task scope rather than universal exclusions. Course descriptions of what “must be true” do not license claiming certainty beyond the actual evidence.
