# Listening notes: recording and retrieval

Provenance: original coaching synthesis from the supplied *TOEFL Listening Course Transcripts.md* and *TOEFL Listening: Notes for Understanding and Fast Retrieval* (`toefl-listening-note-taking-guide.md`, dated 2026-09-14, supplied from Cognitive-Neuroscience-Expert/outputs). Reviewed 2026-09-14. The guide reports earlier missed deadlines and offers retrospective examples from supplied transcripts. Those are historical reports, not this learner's current baseline. Its broader memory/attention explanations do not experimentally validate this protocol or identify a neural deficit.

## Begin with the learner's requested activity

If asked to guide notes, offer one short recording or clearly labelled script exercise. Do not demand a profile or a wrong answer. If notes are supplied, preserve them verbatim before proposing revisions. Ask for an unavailable recording, transcript, question, or timer only when that evidence is needed; meanwhile inspect visible layout and cue placement. Never claim a fact was missing from the audio when only notes are available.

## Record relationships, then stop writing

Listen through a meaningful phrase before compressing it. Prioritize purpose, final decisions, reasons, conditions, event-role details, and examples attached to claims. Preserve words such as only, before, might, and instead when they change the relationship. Separate an initial suggestion, final agreement, and immediate next action.

Use familiar abbreviations, whitespace, or labels in whichever language the learner writes and rereads efficiently. Keep a name or number when its role matters; omit a decorative name, repeated explanation, or long example list when it adds no useful cue. No universal word count or symbol system is required. If writing falls behind, stop and rejoin the current idea. When audio ends, attend to the question instead of polishing notes.

Compare these retrospective teaching examples derived from the guide, not demonstrated first-play notes:

```text
reefs / policy / jobs
```

```text
TALK: reefs
LINK: policy unit
Q&A: careers
```

The second version preserves roles. “What will the question period cover?” maps to Q&A, even though it does not repeat the label's wording. An answer about policy may be familiar and still refer to the wrong part of the event.

Another retrospective example:

```text
WHY: rain -> indoors
suggested: night show
PLAN: museum AM + afternoon show
NEXT: check listings + book
```

The initial evening suggestion must not overwrite the final afternoon agreement. The next action is booking, not going to the museum immediately. Source narratives are not independently verified recordings; do not add timestamps or claim these are the learner's notes.

## Retrieve under the actual limit

Practice question -> relevant cue -> evidence check -> decision. Identify what is requested, go to the matching label, check the option's relationship and qualifiers, then submit before the displayed deadline. Start with one targeted lookup as an adjustable training constraint. Another lookup is useful only if a specific unresolved detail and remaining time justify it. Do not prescribe a fixed lookup duration or assume submitted questions can be revisited.

For a retrieval drill, show the learner's preserved notes and one question; have them identify the cue before choosing. If timing matters, use a real host timer or learner-reported time. Do not invent elapsed seconds. When the user specifically wants live timing and the host cannot provide it, say so and let them use their own timer.

## Locate the contributing stage

| Stage | Evidence to inspect | Next variation |
|---|---|---|
| Listening | Key phrase was not recognized or understood on first play | Fresh short clip; reduce writing, then review sound versus meaning |
| Recording relationships | Nouns exist but roles/conditions were lost | Label two easily confused relationships |
| Finding a cue | Relevant information was recorded but scanning was slow or repeated | Put cues on separate lines; practise question-to-label retrieval |
| Comparing choices | Correct cue found, but time, role, or certainty changed in the answer | Contrast two near paraphrases |
| Deadline | Rechecking continued until no submission | Practise deciding after a targeted lookup with the actual timer |

More than one stage may contribute. “You reported searching repeatedly” is an observation; “unclear cue placement may have contributed” is a hypothesis. Do not infer ADHD, poor working memory, or a stable cognitive weakness.

## Compare methods fairly

When useful, compare no notes, minimal notes, and usual notes over fresh recordings of broadly similar length and difficulty. Rotate conditions across several clips. Replaying the same recording is review, not a clean comparison, because familiarity changes the task. Track correct answers submitted in time and whether the relevant cue was usable; prettier or shorter notes are not the outcome.

Exam notes are temporary retrieval aids. The Active Cognitive Buffer is an optional later study exercise with its own schema. Never require its three pillars or full English sentences during listening. If the learner wants to save a record, use [optional practice records](../practice-records/README.md); keep the original, revision, and retrospective model note separate.
