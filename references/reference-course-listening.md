## Train comprehension by task

| Task | Attend to | A useful short exercise |
|---|---|---|
| Choose a Response | What the speaker is doing: seeking information, requesting, inviting, declining, expressing an opinion, or checking understanding | Choose the reply that satisfies the intention, even without a literal yes/no |
| Conversation | Initial need, reasons, options, change of plan, final agreement, immediate next action, and attitude | Separate what was suggested from what was agreed; match a reply to its implied purpose |
| Announcement | Purpose, relevant time/place, conditions, instructions, changes, likely next action | Attach each detail to its event or role rather than collecting nouns |
| Academic Talk | Concept, definition, examples, contrast, cause/consequence, and conclusion | Match an example to the claim it illustrates or describe the organization |

Question words can narrow a response's information type, but the whole utterance matters: “Do you know when…” usually seeks a time, and an invitation may be declined indirectly by naming a conflicting commitment. Shared vocabulary, tense alone, or a superficial yes/no match is insufficient. An utterance can perform more than one function; judge the best reply in context.

## Separate the attempt from review

For independent practice, play the accessible recording once without showing the transcript, target phrase, or question-specific hints. Ask the learner to indicate completion, then present the comprehension question(s). Keep one manageable recording/set at a time; a requested batch can include several. If the learner requests advance questions or scaffolding, provide them and label the assistance.

After the attempt, replay the relevant segment if accessible, then compare with the transcript. Distinguish failure to recognize a sound sequence from difficulty understanding a recognized phrase, missing a relationship, or selecting a choice. These are hypotheses supported by the learner's report and available evidence, not definitive diagnoses. A transcript alone cannot establish what was audible or how the learner pronounced it; never invent timestamps.

The course suggests listening, listening with text, and repeating. Use this sequence selectively: shadowing a difficult phrase can address sound recognition, but a meaning or retrieval problem may need paraphrase contrast or note retrieval instead. Do not make reproduction mandatory for comprehension or route these requests to the Speaking Listen and Repeat procedure.

## Read the question against the remembered relationship

A main-idea answer must cover the explanation rather than its opening anecdote. Purpose asks why the speaker is communicating; function asks what a particular remark contributes. For a detail, reconnect the fact with the right person, event, or condition. An inference must follow from supplied clues, without an invented motive or outside fact. Attitude must match both the target and the strength of the expression. Neutral wording is not inherently correct, and a strong attitude is correct when supported.

The course's museum example distinguishes an exhibit's hours, its location, tours, and possible waiting. These are related details with different jobs; a tour detail cannot answer a question about why visitors should allow extra time. Do not reconstruct omitted options and call them original course choices.

## Note-taking and delivery

Notes should support comprehension and quick retrieval. Write selectively and stop when writing obscures the next idea. The course's preference for little or no writing on short clips is adjustable, not a rule. It does not justify banning names, numbers, or any note language. “Only you need to read the notes” still requires that you can find the needed cue in time. Load [Listening note-taking](reference-listening-note-taking.md) for recording and retrieval together, and [Audio delivery](reference-audio-delivery.md) before offering playable original listening.

## Source-quality notes

Follow ETS and mark the inconsistent sentence as a transcription problem. Many examples say “pause,” “highlighted,” or refer to choices that are absent. Do not infer real vocal stress, pronunciation, or timing from their text. Approximate clip durations, subtype frequencies, and claims that all speech is slow or actors-only are course characterizations, not current official guarantees. Its neuroscience-related vocabulary in a sample lecture is passage content, not a diagnosis of the learner.
