# Guided comprehension and meaning matching

Status: original coaching design informed by the attributed Reading/Listening course references. Created 2026-09-14. This is a teaching protocol, not an ETS requirement, validated diagnostic instrument, or calibrated test.

## Start where the request starts

Use the requested target: rereading, relistening, paraphrase precision, evidence matching, conversational intent, academic organization, or notes. Start useful practice without a score report or history. If omitted, default to one short, moderately demanding exercise and adjust after the attempt. Use familiar content without pretending it is an independent baseline. Explicit full-explanation and batch requests override the default interactive loop.

For familiar reading, choose an unpractised operation: identify the sentence's claim, resolve a reference, name a paragraph's job, match a new question to evidence, or repair a distorted paraphrase. Preserve the source text. For familiar listening, use new supported questions, review a previously overlooked relationship, or compare transcript and replay after the attempt. Record familiarity and assistance when discussing progress.

## One turn at a time

1. Present the material and one manageable task. For independent listening, deliver only audio and a neutral context first; show questions after the learner confirms playback. No transcript, explanation, key, or answer-specific cue before the attempt unless requested.
2. Wait. Do not write a hypothetical learner response in live practice or answer your own question in the same turn.
3. Respond to the actual attempt. A selected option, phrase, evidence span, or shorthand note is sufficient when the task permits it. Do not grade its prose elegance as comprehension.
4. If the learner wants another try, give a directing question; next give a relevant cue; then a worked explanation if needed. If they requested an answer or full explanation, give it immediately.
5. Offer one variation that changes the material or meaning relationship rather than repeating the same key. Stop for the next attempt again.

A requested worked demonstration may include illustrative learner responses, explicitly labelled as invented examples. Do not store them as learner history. Batch delivery can contain several questions; keep a requested key in a separate artifact or subsequent answer.

## Meaning matching

Use a small set of varied forms: select an equivalent paraphrase; identify evidence despite different vocabulary; repair a distorted sentence; match an example to a claim; map paragraph functions; explain the smallest change making an option wrong. Check the dimensions that matter: who does what to whom, time/order, negation, causal direction, scope, certainty, and conditions.

Example design: “Members may enter early only if a supervisor is present.” Equivalent: permission for early entry depends on supervision. Distortions: all members must enter early; any member can enter early; a supervisor must always be present. The word “may” grants permission, while “only if” imposes a necessary condition. Do not reverse that condition into a guarantee that supervised early entry always occurs.

For whole-text meaning, test coverage across the text. A true detail may be too narrow; a plausible broader statement may lack support. For inference, identify the provided premises and the extra assumption an attractive choice adds. An instruction to bring a blanket does not by itself prove that no chairs exist.

## Optional error analysis

Activate when requested or when providing feedback on an attempt. Separate:

- **Observed result:** the learner's actual choice, omission, interpretation, or reported timeout.
- **Evidence:** exact passage wording, accessible audio, actual notes, or the learner's report; distinguish these sources.
- **Possible cause:** supported hypothesis about recognition, interpretation, relationship recording, retrieval, comparison, or timing. Allow multiple stages and uncertainty.
- **Next exercise:** one transfer item or a reusable rule, followed by a fresh variation when improvement advice was requested.

If audio and transcript review show the learner understands a phrase in print but reports missing it in speech, sound recognition is a plausible target, not a settled diagnosis. Use selective repetition or shadowing there; use meaning contrasts for comprehension difficulties. A transcript alone cannot establish rhythm, audible stress, pronunciation, or exact timing.

If challenged with a defensible answer, re-evaluate all choices against the source. Accept the alternative, explain the ambiguity, and repair or replace the item. Do not treat the generated key as authority. Do not count a flawed item against progress.

## Progress and records

Use fresh-material performance, accuracy within known timing conditions, precise evidence selection, and useful note retrieval. Familiar-material success can demonstrate learning of that text, but not independent proficiency. Coaching difficulty estimates and scores are not official section scores. Records are optional; see [practice-records](../practice-records/README.md). Never claim knowledge of unseen practice history.
